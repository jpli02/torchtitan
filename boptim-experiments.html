boptim Experiments

Nine questions about what the optimisers achieved on CIFAR-10 and on Terminal-Bench, with the Ouro-1.4B exit-gate study kept as an appendix. Each section asks one question, answers it first, then shows the evidence.

Part 1

CIFAR-10 hyperparameter search

Q1 · CIFAR-10

How do the optimisers compare on CIFAR-10?

Answer

Comparable — within about one point. Every optimiser lands in a tight 0.897–0.910 band under the same 10-evaluation budget: gp_hedge is marginally ahead at 0.910, and the LLM optimisers (gpt-5-mini, gpt-5.6-luna, qwen3.5-9b) all reach 0.897–0.900. At n = 2 seeds the ~1-point spread is directional, so on this task they are effectively on par.

Q2 · CIFAR-10

What is the main reason for the gain?

Answer

Search guided by a surrogate model. The GP uses the observed history; the LLMs start from a fixed opening and then exploit it.

  • gp_hedge 0.910 vs LLMs 0.897–0.900. On a smooth 3-D SGD space with 10 evaluations, the GP surrogate searches better than an LLM reading the history.
  • Neither explanation mode nor a stronger model changes it: gpt-5-mini 0.898 → 0.899 with explanations, qwen3.5-9b 0.900 → 0.8966, gpt-5.6-luna 0.899.
  • n = 2 seeds, 1-pt effect. Directional only.
Q3 · CIFAR-10 · Reasoning trace

What does a GPT optimiser's reasoning actually look like?

Answer

One full run — gpt-5.6-luna, explanation on, seed 42 — with the agent's own words at every evaluation. The shape is telling: two exploration probes, then it pins lr and momentum at the midpoint and spends the last seven evaluations line-searching weight decay in noise-sized steps, each one labelled “exploit”. The words say explore; the moves refine a single coordinate.

Read against the search space (lr, momentum, weight decay): lr and momentum never move after eval 2 — only weight decay wiggles across {0.010, 0, 0.0025, 0.00375, 0.003, 0.0022, 0.0027}, steps at the scale of the evaluation noise (σ ≈ 0.0036 accuracy). A GP fitted to the same history ranks these picks at the top of its predicted mean and the bottom of its uncertainty: the model exploits while narrating exploration.

Part 2

Ouro on Terminal-Bench

Q7 · Terminal-Bench

How far does SFT get, and why is pass@1 still low?

Answer

0/80 → 4/80 (0/24 → 5/24 on the 12-task subset, 7/24 with the gated agent), then flat across every recipe.

  • Pass@1 stays low over the 80 tasks: pretrained 0/80, every SFT recipe 2–4/80 (3–5/24 on the subset), always the same two easy tasks.
  • The SFT dataset distribution mismatches the test tasks: generic terminal data and leaderboard trajectories for 38 unrelated tasks; more of it (1k → 20k steps) does not move the score.
  • Verified by overfitting: training on the 12 test tasks themselves (oracle12) jumps 5/24 → 20/24 in 300 steps, so the model can execute these tasks once it is shown their own solutions. But matching the tool distribution with disjoint data does not transfer (Q8): the gap is generalisation capacity, and the fix is a larger base.

Q8 · Terminal-Bench

Data reweighting on the current source doesn't lift pass@1

Answer

No. Concentrating one skill's trajectories to ~40 % of the set and fine-tuning from the v3 checkpoint leaves every task at its 0 baseline. Of 19 skills, 15 moved nothing; 4 tasks flicker to 1/2 — fix-git, path-tracing-reverse, conda-env-conflict-resolution, incompatible-python-fasttext.base_with_hint — right at the ±1 noise floor. Distribution matching, per-task or aggregate, does not move the benchmark.

  • It refines Q6, not contradicts it. The model executes a task's own solution (oracle12, 20/24) but does not transfer skill-matched disjoint data to unseen tasks. The gap is generalisation capacity, not data volume — closing it needs the task's own answer or a larger base, not a better tool mix.
  • One plausible signal in the noise: concentrating C/C++-build data was the run that moved path-tracing-reverse, a write-a-C-program task. Narrow, and still 1 of 2 attempts.
  • Highest-headroom skills moved nothing: perms-arch (2,570 rows), python-env (1,065), web-net, crypto, sci all 0. Not a data-availability artifact.

Q9 · Terminal-Bench

Does scaling to Ouro-2.6B lift pass@1?

Answer

Yes — modestly, and it is capacity, not data. The same 1.4 B backbone at twice the depth (48 layers), trained with the identical tb80sft v3 recipe, reaches 6/24 ungated, 8/24 gated on the 12-task subset and 6/80 on the full set — past the ~5/24 ceiling that held across every 1.4 B recipe (best 5/24 · 6/24 · 4/80). It newly solves crack-7z-hash.easy, a 7z-password task no 1.4 B recipe ever solved.

  • First direct evidence for the Q8 hypothesis: holding data and recipe fixed and only adding capacity unlocks a previously-unreachable task. Gated 8/24 is a new high across all runs.
  • The gain is real but small: 1–2 tasks over the ceiling; easy tasks still dominate and agent_timeout still caps 70/80. Pretrained 2.6 B is 0/80, so SFT is still essential.
  • Per-skill data shaping is still null at 2.6 B: re-running the Q8 sweep on the 2.6 B checkpoint completed 10 of 18 skills (8 aborted when a co-tenant's ~17 GB pushed training out of memory). Eight of the ten moved nothing; two produced a single 1/2 flip each — heterogeneous-dates (data-files set) and conda-env-conflict-resolution (huggingface set) — the same noise-floor pattern as at 1.4 B. csv-to-parquet and fix-pandas-version show as passes but were already solved by the 2.6 B SFT checkpoint. Distribution matching does not help even with the extra capacity — table below.

Q10 · Terminal-Bench

Can a stronger teacher generate new training trajectories?

Answer

Yes, but the yield is low. A rejection-sampling pipeline runs deepseek as the terminus-2 agent on out-of-distribution tasks, grades each attempt with the task's own pytest suite, and keeps only trajectories where every test passes (is_resolved). The deepseek teachers harvested 13 verified trajectories in the first runs and 11 more from a full pass@4 sweep (200 trials over 5 categories × 10 tasks, 5.5 % yield), all disjoint from the 80 eval tasks. Deduplicated across every teacher the corpus is 44 rows — a clean, on-distribution supplement to the fixed leaderboard mix.

  • The all-tests-pass bar is the bottleneck, not the teacher. Both deepseek models frequently land near-misses (7/8, 6/8 tests) but rarely a clean resolve, so per-task yield is a few percent. Sampling pass@4 converts some near-misses; crack-7z-style hard tasks stay at zero.
  • Reasoning vs speed trade-off: deepseek-v4.1-flash (reasoning) resolves but is slow — a 25-min agent cap gives ~4 trials/hr and only 1 unique task in 2 h. deepseek-chat-v3-0324 (non-reasoning) is ~3× faster and converts more near-misses over a full pass@4 run, so it is the workhorse.
  • Tasks are real and gradable: converted from NVIDIA's Nemotron synthetic tasks (CC-BY, unencrypted) into TB 0.2.18 format with explicit output specs and working pytest suites.

Next steps

  • Generate the dataset with deepseek on the previous sources. Run deepseek-chat-v3-0324 as the terminus-2 agent over the task sources already in hand (the converted Nemotron tasks and the TerminalTraj / TB-2 task pool), pass@4, keeping only trajectories that pass each task's own tests, then SFT the 2.6B on the harvested corpus (44 rows so far).

Appendix

Ouro-1.4B exit gate on HumanEval and MBPP

An earlier line of work: tuning the Ouro-1.4B looped-LM exit gate for a speed/accuracy trade-off on HumanEval and MBPP. Kept as an appendix because the gate turned out to be solved-by-default — no tuned setting beat a threshold sweep of the pretrained gate — which is what pushed the effort toward Terminal-Bench.

Q4 · Ouro gate

How much does tuning the Ouro gate gain on HumanEval and MBPP?

Answer
  • HumanEval, vs full depth: 4.0 → 1.229 loops, 3.25× faster, pass@1 0.689 → 0.7195 (+3.0 pt).
  • HumanEval, vs the untuned gate at its best threshold (1.248 loops / 0.7256): 1.02× faster, −0.6 pt. No optimiser beats it.
  • MBPP, full 500: 1.358 loops / 0.528. Vs full depth (4.0 / 0.520): 2.95× faster, +0.8 pt. Vs untuned thr 0.111 (1.440 / 0.536): 1.06× faster, −0.8 pt, inside stderr.
  • By optimiser, vs full depth: gp_hedge 3.25× faster at 0.7195; chatgpt and qwen 3.31× faster at 0.7073.

Q5 · Ouro gate

Why is the gain so limited?

Answer

The pretrained gate is already too strong on HumanEval, and tuning cannot beat it. With no training at all, sweeping its exit threshold gives 1.248 loops at pass@1 0.7256. That sweep already traces the speed–accuracy front. The best tuned point, gp_hedge at 1.229 loops / 0.7195, is 1.02× faster and one problem short; chatgpt and qwen land lower still at 0.7073. No optimiser, no random-init gate and no NAS architecture gets above that line.
